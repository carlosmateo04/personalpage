## 1. Copyright basics for clips: who holds rights, and what licence a paid clipping campaign gives the clipper

### Takeaway
A stream is a stack of separately owned rights (streamer's performance/commentary, the game publisher's audiovisual work, any music), and a campaign licence only passes on what the campaign owner actually controls. The current Content Rewards (Whop) Creator Terms reportedly give clippers only a "limited, revocable, non-exclusive" licence to brand-supplied footage for that one campaign's clip, while the clipper grants the brand and platform a broad perpetual licence back; for GTA footage specifically, Rockstar/Take-Two's published policy tolerates only "non-commercial" posting, which a paid-per-view campaign is not on its face.

### Cited Findings
- Licence to the clipper (current terms, as quoted in search results): "Where a Brand provides footage or assets for a Campaign, the Brand grants you a limited, revocable, non-exclusive license to use that footage only to create and post the Campaign's Clip." [primary, Content Rewards Creator Terms hosted on a Whop app subdomain; version date reported inconsistently as 24 Sep 2026 or 4 Oct 2026] — [Creator terms of service](https://b4e0vdqv6zgqeqj4pfgm.apps.whop.com/terms); same wording repeated in third-party reviews [vendor blog, 2026] — [faceless.my](https://faceless.my/business-models/whop-clipping/)
- Licence the clipper grants: the Clippers Terms say that by uploading a clip the clipper grants Content Rewards Inc. and the specific brand a perpetual, worldwide, royalty-free, sublicensable licence to use, edit, repost, advertise and redistribute the clip [primary; version reported as dated 12 Feb 2025] — [Content Rewards Clippers Terms](https://www.contentrewards.com/terms-of-service/clippers). The older Whop-hosted terms describe the grant to Whop and the seller as worldwide, royalty-free, sublicensable, irrevocable and perpetual [primary; date not verified] — [Whop Content Rewards Terms of Service](https://whop.com/content-rewards-terms-of-service/)
- Two sets of terms exist (older Whop-hosted "Content Rewards Terms of Service" and newer contentrewards.com / Whop-app "Creator Terms"); commentary says campaigns joined under the older Whop-hosted version stay under those prior terms [vendor blogs, 2026] — [fastclips.app review](https://fastclips.app/platforms/whop-content-rewards); [darebay.com review](https://darebay.com/en/earnings/whop-content-rewards-review)
- Whop states the Whop Content Rewards Terms of Service is the "ultimate source of truth" for its Content Rewards policies [primary; date not verified] — [Whop Content Rewards Terms](https://whop.com/content-rewards-terms-of-service/)
- Content Rewards Creator Terms reportedly bar anyone under 18, even with parental consent [primary via search summary; 2025–2026] — [Content Rewards Clippers Terms](https://www.contentrewards.com/terms-of-service/clippers)
- Fees/holds (conflicting): one guide reports a 10% Content Reward fee charged to the seller on payouts and a 10-day hold; another reports a "~7% payout fee" as widely cited [vendor blogs, 2026] — [clipspeed.ai](https://www.clipspeed.ai/blog/whop-content-rewards-clipping-guide.html); [faceless.my](https://faceless.my/business-models/whop-clipping/)
- Game publisher layer (GTA): Rockstar's official policy says "We're happy for fans to upload footage of their gameplay", but defines permitted use as non-commercial: "'Non-commercial' means that you don't make money through the game footage you post or use the material as part of a promotion for a product or service," and "Take-Two Interactive reserves the right to have its copyrighted material taken down at any time." Spoilers, isolated cutscenes, in-game TV/radio shown on their own and leaked pre-release footage are singled out for removal [primary; date not verified] — [Rockstar: Policy on posting copyrighted Rockstar Games material](https://support.rockstargames.com/articles/200153756/Policy-on-posting-copyrighted-Rockstar-Games-material)
- Coverage says the Rockstar policy is expected to apply to GTA 6 at launch, enforcement has historically been uneven, and Take-Two has been demanding removal of leaked GTA VI videos, images and audio [journalism, 2026; date not verified] — [tbreak.com](https://tbreak.com/gta-6-spoilers-rockstar-video-takedown-policy/); [toy-people.com](https://www.toy-people.com/en/?p=114682)
- Music layer: a streamer may hold permission for one use of a composition yet lack the rights to synchronise it with video, keep it in archives, or distribute it "through clips or reposts" [ABA Business Law Today, Sept 2026; commentary] — [When Copyright Claims Hit the Stream](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2026-september/dmca-lessons-for-live-streamers-their-counsel/)
- "Being able to make a clip on Twitch or Kick does not give you permission to repost it for money"; where the streamer allows it, "the copyright question mostly disappears" as to the streamer's own rights [vendor blog, 2026; opinion] — [clipspeed.ai](https://www.clipspeed.ai/blog/can-you-clip-someone-elses-stream.html)

### Inferences
- A campaign licence from a streamer or brand cannot clear rights the licensor does not own. In a GTA 6 stream clip, the streamer can license their face, voice and commentary; the game audiovisuals and soundtrack belong to Take-Two and music rightsholders. The clipper therefore sits on (a) an express but revocable campaign licence for the streamer layer and (b) tolerated-use or fair-use arguments for the game/music layer. **[LAWYER]** to confirm how the campaign terms allocate indemnity for the underlying game/music rights.
- "Revocable" plus "only to create and post the Campaign's Clip" implies no right to keep monetising the clips after the campaign ends or to reuse the footage in other content; the clipper's back-catalogue is at the brand's mercy while the brand keeps a perpetual licence to the clipper's edit. The asymmetry matters for valuing a clip channel as an asset.
- Rockstar's "non-commercial" wording, read literally, excludes paid-per-view clips of GTA footage and clips used "as part of a promotion for a product or service" (e.g., a casino- or brand-sponsored streamer). Practical enforcement has been far looser than the text, but the text gives Take-Two an easy basis for takedowns, particularly in the launch window for GTA 6.

### Gaps
- Could not open the actual Whop / Content Rewards terms, so exact clause numbers, exclusivity wording, platform scope, indemnity, IP-warranty and termination clauses are unverified. The version date conflict (24 Sep vs 4 Oct 2026) is unresolved.
- Individual campaign briefs (per-streamer rules, allowed platforms, music restrictions) are gated behind Whop logins; none were reviewed.
- Whether Rockstar's policy text currently carves out ordinary platform ad-revenue monetisation (a 2013 "clarification" article exists — [VideoGamer](https://www.videogamer.com/news/rockstar-clarifies-gta-5-youtube-copyright-policy) — but its content was not retrieved) and whether Rockstar will publish a GTA 6-specific creator/video policy: not confirmed.
- No source located on whether any streamer-run campaign obtains a sub-licence from the game publisher (unlikely, but unverified).

## 2. Fair use for clips and compilations; legal basis for "30 seconds / change 30% / add captions" rules of thumb

### Takeaway
Fair use is a case-by-case, four-factor defence, not a permission, and no statute, court or Copyright Office guidance supports any seconds-count, percentage-changed or "add captions" safe harbour. Courts through 2026 have repeatedly protected clips interspersed with genuine criticism or commentary (Hosseinzadeh 2017, Hughes 2020, Stebbins 2025, Ted Entertainment v. Saber 2026, Whyte Monkee v. Netflix 2026), but each of those turned on added commentary; a bare highlight re-upload made for per-view pay is the weakest fact pattern, especially after Warhol (2023).

### Cited Findings
- Hosseinzadeh v. Klein (S.D.N.Y. 2017) [court, persuasive]: fair use found for a ~14-minute critical video using ~3 minutes of a 5-minute original; the court expressly said it "is not ruling here that all 'reaction videos' constitute fair use" and distinguished videos that "intersperse short segments of another's work with criticism and commentary" from those "more akin to a group viewing session without commentary." — [U.S. Copyright Office fair use index summary](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/hosseinzadeh-klein-sdny2017.pdf); [Copyright Alliance analysis](https://copyrightalliance.org/fair-use-hosseinzadeh-klein/); [TechCrunch, 23 Aug 2017](https://techcrunch.com/2017/08/23/judge-sides-with-youtubers-ethan-and-hila-klein-in-copyright-lawsuit/?ncid=rss)
- Hughes v. Benjamin, No. 17-cv-6493 (S.D.N.Y. 3 Feb 2020) [court, persuasive]: complaint dismissed at the pleading stage because the defendant's video made of excerpts "plainly fell within the fair use exception"; the court noted the differing target audiences; a §512(f) claim based on the defendant's counter-notice was also dismissed; the plaintiff was later ordered to pay over $38,000 in attorney's fees (fee order July or early Aug 2020 — sources differ) — [Eric Goldman blog, Feb 2020](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2020/02/video-excerpts-qualify-as-fair-use-and-another-512f-claim-fails-hughes-v-benjamin.htm); [Volokh Conspiracy, 6 Aug 2020](https://reason.com/volokh/2020/08/06/sargon-of-akkad-wins-attorney-fees-in-we-thought-she-would-win-sjw-levels-of-awareness-copyright-lawsuit/printer/)
- Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith (U.S. 2023) [settled, Supreme Court]: narrowed how much weight "transformativeness" alone carries under factor one; commentary generally must bear on the original work, and the purpose of the specific use (including commerciality) is weighed — [Copyright Alliance, "Fair Use in a Post-Warhol World: Part II"](https://copyrightalliance.org/?p=11208); [Benesch alert](https://www.beneschlaw.com/insight/supreme-courts-2023-copyright-fair-use-decision-is-not-a-one-hit-wonder/pdf/)
- Whyte Monkee Productions v. Netflix (10th Cir., new opinion 30 Apr 2026) [appellate, binding in the 10th Cir.]: after an earlier panel, citing Warhol, had held the use of a clip was not sufficiently transformative because Netflix added no commentary on the clip itself, the court issued a new 79-page opinion affirming fair use for a short funeral-video clip in Tiger King; "targeting" the original is not mandatory after Warhol, but documentaries get no automatic protection — [Authors Alliance, 1 May 2026](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2026/05/01/tiger-king-2-the-tenth-circuit-sequel-on-fair-use-in-documentary/); [Fenwick](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/tenth-circuit-roars-tiger-king-clip-is-fair-use-netflix-prevails-post-warhol); earlier reversal described at [Frankfurt Kurnit](https://ipandmedialaw.fkks.com/post/102j44f/citing-warhol-10th-circuit-reverses-tiger-king-fair-use-ruling)
- Ted Entertainment, Inc. v. Saber, No. 25-cv-05564 (C.D. Cal. 29 Jun 2026) [court, persuasive]: streamer Denims' livestreamed reaction to an Ethan Klein (H3) video held fair use on a motion for judgment on the pleadings; factor one favoured her because the commentary was critical rather than a repackaging, commerciality of the stream did not outweigh that, and three of four factors "strongly favored" fair use — [U.S. Copyright Office fair use index summary](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/Ted-Entmt-Inc-v-Saber-25-cv-05564-CD-Cal-2026.pdf); [Loeb & Loeb, July 2026](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2026/07/ted-entertainment-inc-v-saber); background on the tentative ruling and the watch-time/chat evidence at [Copyright Lately](https://copyrightlately.com/ethan-klein-denims-reaction-video-fair-use-tentative-ruling/)
- Commentators stress that labelling something a "reaction video" does not by itself make it fair use, and that the pleading-stage win in Saber was "somewhat surprising" though consistent with a growing line of reaction-video cases [law-firm / academic commentary, 2026] — [Vondran Legal](https://www.vondranlegal.com/court-rules-twitch-reaction-video-is-fair-use); [UCI Fair Use Jurisprudence Project](https://ipat.law.uci.edu/fairusejp/)
- Stebbins v. Alphabet (N.D. Cal., July 2025) [court, persuasive]: livestreams containing clips of and commentary on a video about a video game held fair use (reported in 2025 year-end round-ups; exact source page not opened) — [IPWatchdog, 22 Dec 2025](https://ipwatchdog.com/2025/12/22/ai-traditional-copyright-decisions-2025-your-radar/)
- Rules of thumb: "there is no bright line rule for determining when use of a work falls within the scope of the fair use exception"; the U.S. has no 5-, 10- or 30-second rule; duration is one input to one of four factors, and taking the "heart" of a work can weigh against fair use even if short; crediting the creator is legally irrelevant [trade-association / bar commentary; various dates] — [Copyright Alliance, "Top 5 Fair Use Myths on Social Media"](https://copyrightalliance.org/5-fair-use-myths-social-media/); [ABA Landslide, "Debunking Copyright Myths", July/Aug 2018](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/intellectual_property_law/publications/landslide/2018-19/july-august/debunking-copyright-myths/); [NSPA, "The (non-existent) 30-second rule"](https://studentpress.org/nspa/?p=283)
- "Fair use is not permission to use copyrighted content," and because few clip-channel disputes reach a ruling, fair use for clip channels "operates more on platform-level norms than on settled court precedent" [vendor blog, 2026; opinion] — [autoclip.dev](https://autoclip.dev/blog/fair-use-explained-for-clip-channels-2026)
- Platform-side analogue (not law): YouTube's "reused content" monetisation policy still covers clips, compilations and reactions; they are monetisable only with significant original commentary, editing or added value; "inauthentic content" (renamed from "repetitious content") rules took effect 15 Jul 2025, and violations can remove monetisation from the whole channel [journalism, July 2025] — [ppc.land](https://ppc.land/youtube-clarifies-inauthentic-content-policy-changes/); [Plagiarism Today, 8 Jul 2025](https://www.plagiarismtoday.com/2025/07/08/youtube-targets-inauthentic-content/)

### Inferences
- Every pro-defendant case above involved the defendant's own critical commentary layered on the clip. None involved a silent highlight clip posted to earn per-view payments from, or on behalf of, a third party. For unlicensed material in a clip (game footage, music, another streamer's content), a paid clipper would be arguing fair use on the least favourable facts: commercial purpose, little or no commentary, and (for viral moments) taking the "heart".
- Captions, zooms, borders, split-screen gameplay and "30% changed" edits are editing, not commentary; nothing in the retrieved case law or guidance treats them as transformative. They may matter to platform originality/monetisation policies, which is a separate question from copyright liability.
- Where the streamer has licensed the clip, fair use is only needed for third-party layers (game, music). For game footage, publisher tolerance policies do more practical work than fair use, since game-clip fair use has rarely been litigated to judgment.
- The 2025–2026 cases make fair use dismissals at the pleading stage more realistic for commentary content, which lowers litigation cost for commentary-style clip channels but does nothing for bare re-uploads.

### Gaps
- No 2024–2026 decision was found that squarely addresses (a) uncommented highlight clips or (b) fair use of video game footage in short-form clips. Treat as an open question. **[LAWYER]**
- The U.S. Copyright Office's own fair-use overview page (which states there is no formula such as a set number of words/seconds) could not be opened; the "no bright line" point is sourced only to Copyright Alliance/ABA/NSPA commentary here.
- Whether Ted Entertainment v. Saber has been appealed to the Ninth Circuit is unconfirmed; how the fourth (market) factor was resolved was not visible in the summaries.
- EU/UK difference (not sourced this session; from background knowledge, verify): there is no open-ended fair use in the EU or UK; only closed exceptions (quotation, criticism/review, parody/pastiche; UK "fair dealing"), which are generally narrower than US fair use.

## 3. DMCA in practice: takedowns, counter-notices, strikes, repeat-infringer policies, §512(f), and evading content-matching

### Takeaway
A clip channel's real-world exposure is mostly platform enforcement: a DMCA takedown produces a strike, three strikes (YouTube: within 90 days) end the channel, and counter-notices require a sworn statement and consent to federal jurisdiction. §512(f) misrepresentation claims almost never succeed in either direction, and deliberately evading matching systems is a ToS breach and a bad fact for willfulness rather than a workable strategy.

### Cited Findings
- YouTube: a Content ID claim is an automated match with no channel penalty by itself; a copyright strike follows a legal removal request; three strikes within 90 days lead to channel termination; strikes generally expire after 90 days if Copyright School is completed [primary + secondary; current 2026] — [YouTube Help: About copyright removal requests](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13823830?hl=en); [Trademark Engine, 2026](https://www.trademarkengine.com/blog/youtube-copyright-claims-vs-copyright-strikes-creators-need-to-know/)
- YouTube counter-notification: the claimant has 10 US business days to reply with evidence of legal action, otherwise the content may be reinstated; counter-notifications are only for mistake or misidentification, "including exceptions to copyright, such as cases of fair use or fair dealing" [primary] — [YouTube Help: Respond to a counter notification](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12497556). Law-firm sources describe the statutory window as 10–14 business days under 17 U.S.C. §512(g) [commentary] — [Promise Legal counter-notice playbook](https://blog.promise.legal/streamer-dmca-counter-notice-playbook/)
- A Content ID dispute can be escalated by the claimant into a formal takedown, which then carries a strike [law-firm commentary] — [Vondran Legal](https://www.vondranlegal.com/copyright-lawyer-youtube-content-id-copyright-strikes)
- A §512(g) counter-notice requires a statement under penalty of perjury that removal resulted from mistake or misidentification, plus consent to federal court jurisdiction; false contact information in a counter-notice is described as perjury/fraud [commentary] — [Promise Legal](https://blog.promise.legal/streamer-dmca-counter-notice-playbook/); [lawyerinfo.ca guide](https://lawyerinfo.ca/guides/money-taxes-ip/intellectual-property/filing-a-dmca-counter-notice-a-guide-for-canadian-youtubers/)
- §512(f): liability attaches to "knowingly materially misrepresenting" in a notice or counter-notification; Lenz v. Universal (9th Cir.) is the leading case, cert. denied 19 Jun 2017, and the plaintiff there had no significant damages [encyclopedic summary] — [Wikipedia: Lenz v. Universal Music Corp.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenz_v._Universal_Music_Corp.). In Hughes v. Benjamin a §512(f) claim based on the uploader's counter-notice also failed ("another 512(f) claim fails") [commentary, Feb 2020] — [Eric Goldman blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2020/02/video-excerpts-qualify-as-fair-use-and-another-512f-claim-fails-hughes-v-benjamin.htm)
- Twitch: a strike is issued when Twitch receives a DMCA notification about a channel; users are treated as repeat infringers after three strikes, and Twitch reserves the right to terminate regardless of strike count; audio matching scans Clips, and offending clips are removed with strikes; a retraction or compliant counter-notification removes the strike [primary + secondary] — [Twitch DMCA Guidelines](https://legal.twitch.com/legal/dmca-guidelines); [Twitch Creator Camp](https://twitch.tv/creatorcamp/en/paths/copyrights-and-your-channel/what-happens-if-you-violate-copyright-law)
- Meta: a repeat-infringer policy covers IP violations on Facebook profiles and Instagram accounts and accounts that keep posting infringing content may be disabled; Rights Manager is the matching tool for Facebook and Instagram [primary; no numeric strike threshold published in the retrieved text] — [Meta Transparency Center](https://transparency.meta.com/reports/intellectual-property/protecting-intellectual-property-rights); [Meta newsroom, Jan 2023](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/01/helping-creators-and-publishers-manage-intellectual-property/)
- TikTok: its strike-based account enforcement system (announced 2 Feb 2023) applies permanent bans once an account reaches a strike threshold within a policy area, with immediate bans for severe violations; third-party guides say strikes expire after 90 days and that TikTok does not publish the exact IP threshold (one blog claims three strikes per IP type) [primary 2023 + vendor blogs 2026; conflicting] — [TikTok newsroom, 2 Feb 2023](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/supporting-creators-with-an-updated-account-enforcement-system?lang=en); [Soundstripe, 2026](https://www.soundstripe.com/blogs/tiktok-copyright)
- Evasion/circumvention: secondary analysis of YouTube's terms says circumventing restrictions by using another account is a material breach that can justify terminating the whole Google account [third-party clause analysis; date not verified] — [ConductAtlas](https://conductatlas.com/platform/youtube-ads/youtube-terms-of-service/copyright-strike-system-and-account-termination/). YouTube's published "circumvention" rules retrieved here concern Content ID partners abusing manual claiming, not uploaders [primary] — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/3463396)
- Evasion in the wild: an Entain-commissioned study (4–20 May 2026) found clipper accounts altering site names with asterisks (e.g., "St*ke") to avoid automated moderation; reporting notes that when clipper accounts are banned, operators "simply create more accounts" [industry study by an interested party; trade press, 2026] — [blockport.io summary](https://blockport.io/latest-news/ai-personas-fan-accounts-unregulated-uk-gambling/); [iGaming Expert](https://igamingexpert.com/news/affiliates/clipping-threat-black-market-advertising/)
- Low-quality web pages do circulate "how to bypass YouTube copyright" advice and one claims altered videos were treated as "willful circumvention" under the DMCA; this could not be verified and should not be relied on [unreliable] — [spam-like page](https://presentationbuilder.internationaltrucks.com/bypass-youtube-copyright)

### Inferences
- Because paid campaigns reward volume across many accounts, strikes are a structural risk: one rightsholder (e.g., a music label or Take-Two) can strike several clips in a batch and end a channel before a counter-notice plays out. Strike risk, not lawsuits, is the dominant operating constraint.
- A counter-notice is a litigation invitation: it exposes the clipper's identity and requires a sworn good-faith belief of mistake. It is appropriate when the clipper holds a campaign licence from the claimant or a real fair use position, not as a routine tactic. **[LAWYER]** before filing any counter-notice.
- Mirroring, pitch-shifting, cropping or "warming up" replacement accounts after bans evidences knowledge of infringement and breaches platform terms; it would also undermine any good-faith or fair use narrative in litigation.

### Gaps
- Not sourced this session (background knowledge; verify against the statute): statutory damages of $750–$30,000 per work, up to $150,000 per work for wilful infringement (17 U.S.C. §504(c)), available only for timely registered works; whether altering a video to defeat fingerprinting violates 17 U.S.C. §1201 (anti-circumvention) is doubtful and untested, because Content ID is a detection system rather than an access control. **[LAWYER]**
- No primary YouTube, TikTok or Meta policy text was retrieved that specifically addresses uploaders manipulating media to evade matching (YouTube's spam/deceptive-practices and ToS provisions likely apply but were not confirmed).
- TikTok's and Meta's current numeric repeat-infringer thresholds are not published in the retrieved sources.
- No Lumen-database data on takedown volumes against clip channels was retrieved.
- A reported "seven days to resolve strikes before termination" on YouTube appeared only in a secondary source and is unconfirmed.

## 4. Twitch, Kick and YouTube terms on third-party clipping and re-uploading; streamer clip policies

### Takeaway
Platform terms give the platform (not other users) a broad licence to streamer content, so a third party's right to re-upload clips elsewhere comes from the streamer's own permission, an official programme (Kick's Clipping Program, Whop campaigns) or tolerance. Streamer permissions are informal, vary widely, and are revocable.

### Cited Findings
- Twitch: users grant Twitch and its sub-licensees a worldwide, irrevocable, royalty-free, sublicensable licence to user content, including clips, while Twitch claims no ownership [third-party clause analysis of Twitch ToS; date not verified] — [ConductAtlas: Twitch perpetual user content licence](https://conductatlas.com/platform/twitch/twitch-terms-of-service/perpetual-user-content-license/). Streamers remain responsible for everything streamed or uploaded on their channel [primary] — [Twitch Creator Camp](https://twitch.tv/creatorcamp/en/paths/copyrights-and-your-channel/why-are-copyright-laws-important-for-me-to-think-about-when-i-stream)
- Twitch streamers can restrict clip creation to followers/subscribers or switch off viewer-made clips entirely in Clip Settings [creator guide; date not verified] — [Creator Handbook](https://www.creatorhandbook.net/how-to-clip-on-twitch/)
- Kick: its terms reportedly grant Kick a royalty-free, sublicensable, transferable, perpetual, irrevocable, worldwide licence to user content "in any and all media or distribution methods"; infringement claims are processed under DMCA procedures [third-party clause analysis; the excerpt appeared to contain Twitch-branded wording, so reliability is doubtful] — [ConductAtlas: Kick user content licence](https://conductatlas.com/platform/kick/kick-terms-of-service/user-content-license/)
- Kick runs an official Kick Clipping Program (made official in late 2025): streamers apply and clippers then find and share clips on their behalf [esports press; date not verified] — [win.gg](https://win.gg/kick-clipping-program/). Kick's internal data reportedly showed its clipping programme generated over three billion views in September 2025 alone [weak source] — [Publish0x post](https://www.publish0x.com/decentralised-africa/how-ai-paid-clippers-and-crypto-igaming-rewired-the-attentio-xokrpmj)
- Streamer-funded clipping at scale: Tubefilter (29 Apr 2026) reports Kick streamer N3on spending millions on stream clippers; search summaries cite rates of about $40–$50 per 100,000 views [journalism] — [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2026/04/29/n3on-spending-millions-stream-clippers-tiktok-kick/amp/)
- Streamer practice varies: many creators publish clipping guidelines, run official clip channels or grant blanket permission subject to credit; others want no re-uploads, or object to monetised re-uploads and out-of-context or misleading edits; advice is to remove a clip on request [vendor blog, 2026; opinion] — [clipspeed.ai](https://www.clipspeed.ai/blog/can-you-clip-someone-elses-stream.html)
- YouTube: fan channels must state fan status explicitly in the channel name or handle (description alone is insufficient), and "posing as another's channel and reuploading their content" violates the impersonation policy (rules effective 21 Aug 2023) [primary + journalism] — [YouTube Impersonation policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801947?hl=en); [TechCrunch, 23 Jun 2023](https://techcrunch.com/2023/06/23/youtubes-guidelines-require-channels-disclose-they-are-fan-accounts/)
- An unverified marketing blog asserts that Kick's terms are "generally permissive regarding clips compared to Twitch" and that monetising clips on TikTok/YouTube is a grey area depending on the streamer [vendor blog; unverified] — [autoclip.dev](https://autoclip.dev/blog/kick-clipping-guide-2026)

### Inferences
- The platform licence clauses run from streamer to platform. Nothing retrieved shows Twitch, Kick or YouTube granting other users a licence to re-upload a streamer's content off-platform; the in-platform "Clip" button creates a platform-hosted clip, not an export licence.
- The safest documented permission routes are, in order: a written campaign licence (Whop/Content Rewards or direct), the platform's official programme (Kick Clipping Program), then a streamer's published blanket policy. Discord rules or verbal "clip me" statements are evidence of an implied licence but are revocable and usually silent on monetisation.

### Gaps
- No primary-source clip policy for any named large streamer (Kai Cenat, xQc, Asmongold, Adin Ross, etc.) was retrieved; these typically live in Discord servers or stream panels and were not accessible. The report should not cite specific streamers' policies without checking them directly.

## 5. Advertising disclosure for paid clips: FTC Endorsement Guides (2023), 2024–2026 enforcement, who is liable; UK and EU equivalents

### Takeaway
A clipper paid per view by or for the person or brand featured has a material connection that must be disclosed clearly inside the video experience; platform paid-partnership labels are recommended in addition to, not instead of, the clipper's own disclosure. As of late 2026 no FTC action aimed specifically at the clipping model was found, but the first private suits over a Whop-run clipping campaign (Polymarket, June 2026) and repeated press findings that clipping campaigns almost never carry disclosures make this the most exposed compliance area. The UK regime (DMCC Act, CMA direct fines) is stricter on penalties.

### Inferences
- A clipper paid per view by a streamer, a platform (Kick) or a brand is an endorser with a material connection; campaign briefs that say "don't make it feel like an ad" or forbid brand names in handles (as alleged in NACA v. Polymarket) are instructions to do the opposite of what the Guides require and create exposure for brand, agency/marketplace and clipper alike.
- Practical compliant pattern for short-form: on-screen text such as "Ad" / "Paid by [streamer/brand]" visible at the start of the clip, plus the platform's paid-partnership toggle, plus #ad at the start of the caption. A hashtag buried after "more" or a bio-only statement is not enough on the commentary above.
- Enforcement probability against an individual small clipper remains low (FTC has historically pursued brands, agencies and prominent endorsers), but (a) private consumer-protection suits now name campaign organisers, (b) Whop terms let brands withhold payment for non-disclosure, and (c) UK-audience clips fall under a regime with direct fining power.
- Disclosure reduces reach on some platforms and conflicts with the "organic" premise of clipping; a compliant operation should expect lower per-clip performance than undisclosed competitors — a feasibility cost, not just a legal footnote.

## 7. Business basics: payouts, US tax reporting for 2026, need for an LLC, KYC

### Takeaway
Payouts require identity verification on Whop, clip income is self-employment income that is taxable whether or not a form arrives, and the 2026 reporting thresholds are $2,000 for Form 1099-NEC/MISC and $20,000 plus 200 transactions for Form 1099-K. An LLC is optional for tax purposes (a single-member LLC is disregarded) and its value is liability segregation, which is a state-law question.

### Cited Findings
- 1099-NEC / 1099-MISC: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act raised the reporting threshold from $600 to $2,000 for payments made in 2026, indexed for inflation from 2027; the backup-withholding threshold moves to $2,000 as well [tax-trade press, July 2025 onward] — [CPA Practice Advisor, 30 Jul 2025](https://cpapracticeadvisor.com/2025/07/30/one-big-beautiful-bill-act-changes-1099-thresholds/165863/); [Patriot Software](https://www.patriotsoftware.com/?p=81429); [NATP](https://www.natptax.com/news-insights/blog/form-1099-k-backup-withholding-rules-are-officially-final/)
- 1099-K: OBBBA restored the $20,000 and 200-transaction threshold for third-party settlement organisations (retroactive to 2022), not inflation-indexed; one source notes payment-card transactions remain reportable at any amount, the threshold applying to third-party network transactions [tax-trade press, 2025–2026] — [TaxAct](https://blog.taxact.com/4-common-misconceptions-about-the-new-1099-k-reporting-threshold/); [OnPay](https://onpay.com/insights/1099-reporting-threshold-updates/); [1-800Accountant](https://1800accountant.com/blog/irs-1099-reporting-changes-2026)
- Conflicting/outdated claim: a Fanvue help article still cites a $600 1099-K threshold from 2026; this pre-dates or ignores OBBBA and is contradicted by the sources above [platform help page; outdated] — [Fanvue help](https://help.fanvue.com/en/articles/7860548-do-i-need-a-w9-or-1099-form-for-my-taxes)
- All income is taxable even if no 1099 is issued; self-employment tax applies once net self-employment earnings reach $400, at 15.3% on 92.35% of net earnings, reported on Schedule C and Schedule SE; tax is on profit after expenses [consumer tax guides, 2025–2026] — [TurboTax blog](https://blog.turbotax.intuit.com/self-employed/do-creators-pay-taxes-on-money-earned-51416); [TaxAct](https://blog.taxact.com/?p=161783); [Credit Karma](https://www.creditkarma.com/tax/library/career-taxes/do-influencers-pay-taxes/)
- LLC: not needed to owe or pay self-employment tax; a single-member LLC is disregarded for federal income tax by default, so the owner files the same Schedule C and pays the same self-employment tax [tax blog, 8 Oct 2026] — [beancount.io](https://beancount.io/blog/2026/10/08/content-creator-influencer-tax-planning-s-corp-deductions-sponsorship-guide)
- Forms: US creators typically provide a W-9 before payment and receive a 1099-NEC if payments reach the threshold; non-US individuals provide a W-8BEN (W-8BEN-E for entities) [vendor guides, 2026] — [SideShift](https://sideshift.app/blog/ugc-creator-tax-forms); [Payouts.com](https://payouts.com/resources/1099-threshold-2026-creator-payments)
- Whop KYC: Whop's documentation describes identity verification (KYC for individuals, KYB for businesses) for accounts receiving payouts, with statuses pending/approved/rejected/action_required, possible follow-up requests such as a bank statement, and reuse of prior Sumsub verification via share token [primary docs; 2026, date not verified] — [Whop docs: verification overview](https://docs.whop.com/developer/verification/overview.md); [Whop docs: set up payouts](https://docs.whop.com/manage-your-business/manage-payouts/set-up-payouts.md)

### Inferences
- With the 1099-NEC threshold at $2,000 and 1099-K at $20,000/200, many small clippers will receive no information return for 2026; the income is still reportable, and KYC'd payout records mean it is traceable. Budget roughly 15.3% self-employment tax plus income tax on net profit and make quarterly estimated payments once earnings are material. **[Tax adviser]**
- Which form applies depends on how the payer is characterised: a brand or agency paying a clipper directly for services would issue a 1099-NEC; a marketplace acting as a third-party settlement organisation would issue a 1099-K. Whop's own practice was not confirmed.

This is general legal background, not sourced this session. **[LAWYER]**

## 8. Right of publicity, name and likeness in channel names, thumbnails and titles; impersonation policies

### Takeaway
The binding constraint is platform impersonation policy rather than litigation: YouTube requires fan/clip channels to say so in the channel name or handle and can terminate channels that pose as the creator. Right of publicity is state law requiring commercial use of identity without consent; no case applying it to a clip channel was found, and a campaign licence or streamer permission largely removes the issue as long as the channel does not imply it is official.

### Cited Findings
- YouTube impersonation policy: no unauthorised impersonation of a person, entity or channel that may mislead viewers, including "deceptively copying the branding, content, or usernames" and using AI to copy voice or likeness; fan channels must state so "explicitly in your channel name or handle"; a channel that claims fan status only in the description, or that poses as another's channel and re-uploads their content, violates the policy; violations may lead to channel or account termination [primary; fan-channel rule effective 21 Aug 2023] — [YouTube Impersonation policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801947?hl=en); [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/youtube-fan-accounts-will-soon-need-a-disclosure-in-the-channel-name-or-handle-035015131.html); [TechCrunch, 23 Jun 2023](https://techcrunch.com/2023/06/23/youtubes-guidelines-require-channels-disclose-they-are-fan-accounts/)
- Right of publicity generally requires (1) use of a person's name or likeness, (2) for commercial purposes, (3) without consent; it covers name, likeness, voice, signature, nickname and other identifying characteristics; it is primarily state law and particularly strong in California [law-firm commentary, 2024–2026] — [Traverse Legal](https://www.traverselegal.com/blog/what-is-right-of-publicity/); [Mondaq, "Right of Publicity Revisited in the AI Era"](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/trademark/1800622/right-of-publicity-revisited-in-the-ai-era)
- First Amendment tension is significant and plaintiffs face a heavy burden where the use is expressive; EFF frames the issue as whether celebrities get "a veto right over creative works that depict them" [advocacy / commentary] — [EFF: Right of Publicity](https://www.eff.org/issues/right-publicity)
- Closest creator-economy dispute found: Anthony Fantano's counsel alleged false endorsement over a publisher's use of his audio clip; the publisher relied on a platform sound-library licence; reported as unresolved [law-firm commentary; date not verified] — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/copyright/1667496/50-cents-fight-over-his-image-highlights-challenges-in-copyright-and-right-of-publicity-lawsuits)
- Conversely, campaigns that forbid the brand's name in account handles to appear organic (alleged in NACA v. Polymarket) show the opposite problem: concealment of affiliation rather than impersonation [law-firm commentary, 2026] — [Kelley Drye](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/all-bets-are-off-as-polymarket-faces-lawsuit-over-influencer-campaigns)
- Kick's terms reportedly include a perpetual, irrevocable content "and likeness" licence to Kick (i.e., to the platform, not to third-party clippers) [third-party clause analysis; reliability doubtful] — [ConductAtlas](https://conductatlas.com/platform/kick/kick-terms-of-service/perpetual-irrevocable-content-and-likeness-license/)

### Inferences
- Naming convention that satisfies YouTube's rule and avoids false-endorsement arguments: "[Streamer] Clips (fan channel)" or a neutral brand name, never the streamer's exact handle, logo or avatar alone; say "unofficial" unless the campaign terms authorise "official clips" branding.
- Using the streamer's face in thumbnails and name in titles to identify whose content the clip shows is descriptive and, with campaign permission, low risk; risk rises with (a) misleading or fabricated-quote thumbnails, (b) AI voice/likeness, (c) using the streamer's identity to sell an unrelated product or sponsor (commercial appropriation / false endorsement under state law and Lanham Act §43(a)).
- The disclosure duty in section 5 and the impersonation rules point the same way: the channel should be openly a paid/affiliated or fan clip channel, neither pretending to be the streamer nor pretending to be an unpaid fan.

### Gaps
